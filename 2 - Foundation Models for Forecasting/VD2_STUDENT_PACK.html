3. Compare three supplied methods

Method Complete recipe
Seasonal naive (seasonal_naive) Repeat the last complete training week across all 42 forecast days. This is the simple benchmark.
Weekday mean (weekday_mean) Estimate seven weekday means from the last 56 training days, including observed zero-sales days. This is the current-process comparison.
TimesFM (timesfm) Use the supplied fixed-weight forecasts made from the last 84 observed training days. No local fitting or future inputs were used.

All methods forecast the same store-days. Different history lengths are part of these supplied recipes, so the results compare complete methods rather than isolating architecture. A 42-day seasonal-naive forecast repeats the last training week six times; it never reads actual sales from inside that horizon.

The foundation file uses google/timesfm-2.5-200m-transformers at revision 5a9806b9b291fad9233b5249d88263f1846304d3. In this pinned implementation, its point-output field selects the median, despite its library name mean_predictions. The snapshot also includes the 0.10, 0.80, and 0.90 quantiles. Document that you evaluated supplied forecasts; do not claim you trained or ran the model yourself. Running inference independently is optional and earns no additional points by itself. The supplied file fixes the required comparison across students.

The calendar assumption

For the main comparison, treat recorded Open as the planned schedule known at each origin. On a planned closed day, set every method's prediction to zero; set both bounds of the foundation interval to zero too. The script also reports the raw predictions before this adjustment.

The data do not verify that this schedule was actually known six weeks in advance. This is an explicit classroom information assumption. If it would not hold in practice, the adjusted comparison would not establish deployable performance. The raw comparison helps show how much that assumption changes the conclusion. Zeroing a known closure is different from using future Sales or Customers to improve a forecast.

MAE is mean absolute error in the Sales measure. WAPE is total absolute error divided by total absolute actual sales on the same rows. It is undefined if that denominator is zero; individual zero-sales rows stay in the numerator. Lower values are better. The script pools error and actual sums when combining windows; it does not average percentages with different denominators.

In analysis.md, record the method you select and two or three sentences explaining the validation evidence. The default is lowest combined validation MAE. On an exact tie, prefer seasonal naive, then weekday mean, then TimesFM. A simpler method with a small MAE disadvantage is also acceptable if you explain the tradeoff. No particular winner is required.

Record the choice before evaluating the last window. A dated note is sufficient; Git commit counts and timestamps are not grading criteria. If you already evaluated first, state what happened and explain its limitation. Do not invent an earlier decision record.

Check 1 — Information and dates

Explain why the training week can be repeated but future actuals cannot be read to update the original forecast. Check one store's forecast day 8 against its last training week. Explain the excluded Customers field and the stated Open assumption. Show that the final comparison has 840 rows per method, with no duplicate forecast keys or missing predictions.

Check 2 — Error measures and groups

Report the three final MAEs and WAPEs. Write WAPE's numerator and denominator in words, including which rows they describe. Check one method's absolute-error sum divided by its actual-sales sum independently with a calculator or spreadsheet. Compare the five-store and fifteen-store groups. Explain why this sample does not support a claim about every store or about staff headcount.

Check 3 — Uncertainty and changing origins

The script supplies 1,000 paired bootstrap samples with seed 0, resampling stores with replacement and retaining all 42 days within a selected store. Both methods are scored on the same resampled stores; subtract their MAEs inside each resample. Report the chosen method's difference from seasonal naive and from weekday mean, omitting a self-comparison if one is your choice. If you chose seasonal naive, discuss the strongest validation alternative against it as well.

A negative difference favors the method named first. An interval containing zero leaves its direction uncertain; it does not establish that the methods are equivalent. These are exploratory intervals for fixed forecasts and a small, selected store sample. They omit new model fitting, selection uncertainty, future shocks, and shared calendar dependence between stores. There is no multiple-comparison adjustment. Also show the three windows' results and explain whether the size or order of the differences changes. Three windows are descriptive evidence, not a long-run performance guarantee.

Check 4 — Calendar and forecast intervals

Compare raw and schedule-adjusted error for your chosen method. It is acceptable for the adjustment to make no difference; explain why rather than manufacturing an effect. Separate the assumption that the schedule was known from the measured change in retrospective error.

For the supplied TimesFM arm, explain the 0.10–0.90 central 80% interval, its observed coverage, and its average width. Report coverage on all days and open days separately, with counts. Check interval ordering before interpreting coverage. Closed days receive [0, 0] under the schedule assumption and can inflate pooled coverage. A coverage rate above 80% does not automatically mean a better range; width and the decision's cost matter too. The required interval check applies to the foundation arm even if you selected another method. You do not need to construct additional interval models.

The forecast interval describes an outcome range. The bootstrap interval in Check 3 describes uncertainty in a model difference. Do not confuse them or sum daily forecast quantiles into a six-week total-demand quantile.
