Session 4 — Which Forecast Should the Planning Team Use?

MKTG 6620 · Monday, September 21, 2026

A planning team needs forecasts for the next 15 days. Should it repeat last week, average several recent weeks, or use a pretrained forecasting model? We will build the comparison, check which observations its averages describe, and decide what evidence would justify trying a different planning method.

The decision and the three methods

Rina Kapoor, a fictional planning executive, wants to know whether a new forecast is useful enough to pilot. We use the public Favorita store-sales example, preserving its original store, product-family, date, and sales fields. These observations are not records from Rina's company.

A time series is a sequence of observations indexed by time. Here one series is one store and product family. A forecast origin is the first day being predicted; everything supplied to the forecasting method must be available before that day. The horizon is how far ahead the forecast must reach. A 15-day forecast answers a different planning question from tomorrow's forecast.

Method What it learns or uses What to expect it to miss
Seasonal naive Repeat the last complete training week across all 15 days. No fitted parameters. A recent unusual week is repeated.
Weekday mean Estimate seven averages from the last eight weeks: one for each weekday. This is a simple fitted model. Sudden changes and effects not captured by weekday.
TimesFM A foundation model pretrained on other time series. Give its fixed weights the previous 112 days as context; perform no local training. Future events absent from that history; suitability for this population still needs checking.

A foundation model has already been trained. Zero-shot forecasting here means we use its existing weights without fitting them on this class file. It still needs a carefully chosen history, evaluation period, and output. These are three complete forecasting recipes with different history lengths; the comparison cannot isolate the effect of architecture alone.

The agent can write and execute the code, while you specify the task and check the evidence.

The measures, before we compute them

Term Meaning and limitation
Error Actual sales minus forecast sales. Absolute error ignores its sign.
MAE Mean absolute error. Its units match the target. Our pooled value averages store-family-days; large-volume families contribute more absolute error.
WAPE Sum of absolute errors divided by sum of absolute actual sales, on the same rows. Report it as a percentage. Undefined when the denominator is zero; otherwise zero-sales rows still contribute their errors.
MAPE Mean of each row's absolute error divided by its actual. Undefined at a zero actual. Dropping zeros changes the population being described.
MASE A series' test MAE divided by its average absolute seven-day difference in training. Undefined when that training denominator is zero. Its value of 1 compares with that training scale, not necessarily with the baseline's error in this test window.
Quantile A forecasted threshold for a stated share of outcomes, such as the 0.80 quantile. Check that lower quantiles are no larger than higher ones.
Prediction interval A range for a future outcome. The 0.10 to 0.90 quantiles describe a nominal central 80% interval.
Coverage The observed share of outcomes inside an interval, or below a stated quantile. State which rows were counted.
Paired uncertainty interval An interval on a difference between methods, computed on common resamples. This is distinct from a prediction interval.

For a small invented example, actuals [0, 10, 20] and forecasts [2, 8, 25] give absolute errors [2, 2, 5], MAE 3, and WAPE 30%. MAPE is undefined. Dropping the zero produces MAPE 22.5% on two of three rows. That smaller number answers a different question. Both numbers can be computed correctly; their denominators tell us what they mean.

Pooled central coverage is above 80%, while dense-series coverage is below 80%. All-zero series contribute easy [0, 0] intervals. For discrete outcomes, a quantile can have coverage above its nominal level because many observations tie at the same value. Coverage is evidence to inspect alongside interval width and decision costs; a nominal label alone is not validation.

A supported class conclusion is to consider TimesFM for a limited comparison in the planning process, retain the simple forecast as a benchmark, and check more future periods, interval behavior, and operational costs. Another supported action is to retain the simpler method while collecting that evidence. The data alone cannot tell us how much inventory to purchase or how many people to schedule.

Practice 3 — Forecast day eight and an absent date

Worked answer: [10,20,30,40,50,60,70,10,20,30,40,50,60,70,10]. Days 1, 8, and 15 all use the same first day of the final training week. Practice 4 — When an order decision needs a quantile

Use a fictional one-day inventory decision. Demand is 0 with
probability 0.5 and 10 with probability 0.5. Each unit of unmet demand
costs $8; each unsold unit costs $2. Compare ordering 5 with ordering
10 by enumerating both outcomes. Compute 8/(8+2). Explain why this
example does not justify summing 15 daily 0.80 quantiles into a
0.80 quantile of total demand.

Worked answer: expected demand is 5. Order 5: expected loss is 0.5 × (5 × $2) + 0.5 × (5 × $8) = $25. Order 10: expected loss is 0.5 × (10 × $2) + 0.5 × $0 = $10. The cost ratio is 0.80 and the smallest demand value whose cumulative probability reaches 0.80 is 10. This is the optimal order for this simplified one-period problem, with no capacity or other cost terms. It is an invented demand distribution, not a finding about Favorita's observed sales.

For 15-day total demand, the optimal order would use the distribution of the total, including dependence between days. Marginal daily quantiles do not supply that joint information. Quantiles cannot generally be added. A calibrated daily forecast is a useful input, but it does not by itself solve replenishment.
