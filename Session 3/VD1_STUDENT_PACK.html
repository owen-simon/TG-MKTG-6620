1. The business decision

Devon Achebe, VP of Customer Retention at Summit Telecom, is considering a retention offer. Summit Telecom and Devon are fictional. The team can contact at most 20% of customers. Devon wants to know:

“Does a prediction model identify a more useful contact list than our contract rule? Is the potential value large enough to justify a pilot?”

Use the public Telco customer-churn file as a classroom example. Keep its original meaning: Churn = Yes records that a customer left. Telephone and internet services remain telephone and internet services. The file is an example dataset, not verified records from Summit Telecom or evidence about a retail membership program.

A high churn probability does not tell us whether an offer will persuade that customer to stay. The analysis helps us form a list to test. It cannot establish the effect of contacting people.

3. Compare three methods

Use these three methods and the supplied settings. You can earn every point with this comparison alone.

Method What it does
Contract rule (contract) Assign each customer the training-data churn rate for their contract category. This is the simple baseline.
Logistic regression (logistic) Combine the seven inputs into a predicted churn probability.
Boosted trees (trees) Combine a sequence of small decision trees using the same inputs.

A baseline is the simple option against which the additional modeling must be assessed. A predicted probability is the model's estimate of how often churn would occur among comparable customers; it needs checking against observed outcomes.

The script uses logistic regression with C=1 and boosted trees with 100 trees, depth 2, and learning rate 0.1. It keeps the original class proportions. Numeric scaling and category encoding are learned only from training rows. These settings are supplied so that your time goes into understanding the comparison.

Keep model choice separate from final evaluation

The script divides rows into 60% training, 20% validation, and 20% final test, with rounding to whole rows. Both split steps use random seed 0 and keep similar churn proportions in each part.

  • Training rows teach the rule and models, including preprocessing.
  • Validation rows help you choose the method to carry forward.
  • Final-test rows evaluate that recorded choice afterward.

This random split estimates performance on records resembling this example file. It does not demonstrate performance in a later campaign or another company. Do not repeatedly adjust settings to improve final-test results.

AUC measures ranking: how often does a randomly selected churner receive a higher score than a randomly selected non-churner? Ties receive half credit. AUC 0.50 means no ranking advantage. AUC 0.80 does not mean that 80% of all customers were classified correctly.

In analysis.md, record your selected method and two or three sentences explaining the validation evidence. The default is the highest validation AUC; on an exact tie choose the simpler method in the table above. Choosing a simpler method despite a small AUC disadvantage is also acceptable if you explain that tradeoff. You do not need a particular winner.

5. Evaluate the choice and check the results

Replace logistic below with your recorded choice: contract, logistic, or trees.

python VD1_analysis.py evaluate --csv churn.csv --out outputs --choice logistic

The script reconstructs the same training fits with the same settings; it does not refit on validation or final-test rows. It produces the final comparison for all three methods, including the alternatives you did not choose. Keep your original choice visible if the final-test ordering differs.

The four checks to explain

Check What to show and explain
Data and separation Confirm the row/outcome counts and the treatment of blank charges. Use split_rows.csv to check that no source row belongs to two partitions. Locate where the code restricts fitting to training rows.
Ranking and the contact list Report all three final-test AUCs. Also report the observed churn rate among each method's highest-scoring 20%, alongside the overall test churn rate. Explain which quantity answers the contact-list question.
Uncertainty in a comparison Report the supplied AUC intervals and the paired AUC differences from the contract rule. Explain the chosen method's comparison with the baseline; if you chose the baseline, explain the strongest alternative's comparison with it.
Probability accuracy For your chosen method, compare predicted and observed churn rates overall, within its top-20% list, and across the supplied probability groups. Identify any meaningful mismatch and any group too small to support a confident conclusion.

For the list, the script rounds the contact count down to stay within 20%. Ties are broken using a fixed random order that does not use churn outcomes. This matters especially for the contract rule, which gives many customers identical scores. A different tie order can change that rule's list.

Calibration means agreement between probabilities and observed frequencies in comparable groups. An overall average can look right even when the highest-risk group is overpredicted. AUC measures order and cannot establish that probabilities are accurate. You only need to diagnose the supplied probabilities; building a recalibration model is outside the required work.

A bootstrap interval comes from repeatedly sampling test rows with replacement and recomputing the statistic. The script supplies 1,000 samples and reports the middle 95% of the resulting AUCs. For a difference, it scores both methods on the same sampled rows and subtracts inside each sample. That is a paired interval. You may use this code; explain its logic in your own words.

An interval that includes zero leaves the direction of the difference uncertain. It does not prove that the methods are equal or that a useful difference is impossible. These intervals describe test-sample uncertainty for the fixed models under a row-independence assumption. They do not include uncertainty from training a new model, future changes, or the effectiveness of an offer. The three comparisons are exploratory, with no adjustment for multiple comparisons; a barely positive interval is limited evidence for a broad claim of superiority.

You are not required to write a bootstrap routine, repeat ten split seeds, rebalance the data, search six models, or produce a failed AI answer. More models or more prompts earn no additional credit.

6. Assess the business value under explicit assumptions

Evaluate the same top-20% list at each of three assumed save rates. You are assessing a candidate campaign, not required to use the entire budget or contact customers whose estimated value is negative.

Planning assumption Value
Contribution from one additional customer retained, before the offer $81
Offer cost for each additional retention $15
Net value per additional retention $66 = $81 − $15
Cost of each contact, whether successful or not $6.20
Share of otherwise-churning customers retained because of contact Try 10%, 15%, and 20%

All five rows are fictional assumptions. The simplified offer-cost assumption excludes payments to customers who would have stayed anyway. A real offer paid to those customers would require additional cost terms.

Let r be the observed churn rate in the chosen method's top-20% final-test list. It is a historical estimate of that group's churn risk. Let s be the assumed share of otherwise-churning customers saved by the offer. The data do not estimate s.

Scenario net value per 1,000 contacts = 1,000 × (r × s × $66 − $6.20).

Here r × s is the assumed number of additional retentions per contact; multiply by $66 and subtract the contact cost. For illustration only, if r is 60% and s is 15%, the calculation is 1,000 × (0.60 × 0.15 × $66 − $6.20) = −$260. That is hypothetical value under the assumptions, not measured campaign profit.

The script supplies this table for all three lists. Check one row independently with a calculator or spreadsheet. For the chosen list, also report:

Break-even save rate = $6.20 / (r × $66).

This is the share of otherwise-churning customers the offer must save to cover contact costs under this cost model. At r = 60%, it is about 15.7%. If r is zero, there is no feasible break-even save rate; if the result exceeds 100%, the campaign cannot break even under these assumptions.

Explain whether changing s changes your recommendation. The list stays fixed across scenarios. Sampling error in r, future customer differences, and the unknown offer effect remain limitations. A well-supported recommendation to defer the campaign, use the simple rule, or run a small randomized pilot can all earn full credit.
