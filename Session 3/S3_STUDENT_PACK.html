A marketing team can contact only a small share of its customers. It wants to put people likely to respond near the top of the list. A model can help, but three different checks are needed:

  1. Does it rank likely responders above unlikely responders?
  2. Does it use information available when the list must be made?
  3. When it predicts a probability, does that number agree with observed response rates?

We use real bank-marketing data. The recorded outcome is whether a client subscribed to a term deposit. We do not relabel those outcomes as retail renewals or churn, and we do not claim the data measure the effect of making a call.

The ideas you need

Classification means predicting a category. Here the categories are “subscribed” and “did not subscribe.” The recorded answer is the label. The inputs, sometimes called features, are the facts the model uses to make its prediction.

Logistic regression combines inputs into a score and converts it to a number between zero and one. It is a useful starting model because it is relatively simple. A decision tree asks a sequence of questions about the inputs and gives different predictions to the resulting groups. Boosted trees build a sequence of trees, each helping improve the predictions made so far. More complicated models can find patterns the simpler model misses; whether that helps on new records is something we measure.

Training data teach the model. Validation data help choose between our two models. Final-test data assess that choice afterward. If we repeatedly change the model to improve its final-test result, that sample has become another model-selection sample. Keep a record of the choices made before opening it.

AUC measures ranking: compare a randomly selected subscriber with a randomly selected non-subscriber. How often does the subscriber receive the higher score? Ties receive half credit. An AUC of 0.50 means no ranking advantage. An AUC of 0.69 does not mean that 69% of all customers were classified correctly.

Response rate among the top 10% answers a more direct business question: among the records the model ranks highest, what fraction actually subscribed? We use the top 10% as a classroom contact-capacity assumption, not a fact about the original bank's budget.

Calibration means that predicted probabilities agree with observed frequencies across comparable groups. Among many records predicted around 30%, roughly 30% should respond. Matching the overall average is not enough; the high-scoring group can still be overpredicted.

A confidence interval shows uncertainty in a reported estimate under a stated sampling method. Here we use a bootstrap: repeatedly sample evaluation rows with replacement, recompute a result, and report the middle 95% of those results. For a model difference, calculate the difference inside each common sample. This is a paired interval. An interval that includes zero does not establish an improvement; it also does not prove the models are equivalent.

The data and the decision

Imagine you are advising a bank's marketing manager about a small future calling pilot. Before recommending a model, show how it performs on historical records and what still needs testing. The bank data contain 41,188 records and 21 columns, including 4,640 yes and 36,548 no outcomes. These are historical campaign records, not a list of 41,188 confirmed unique customers currently eligible for calls.

Always predicting “no” matches 88.73% of the labels in the full file. That descriptive count explains why accuracy alone is a weak way to choose a calling list. A predicted label and a contact policy are separate decisions: predicting “no” does not itself mean nobody will be called.

The comparison's intervals resample evaluation records while keeping the fitted models fixed. They describe uncertainty from which records are evaluated. They do not include the uncertainty from retraining on different data, customer dependence, a future economic change, or our choice of inputs. The untouched test sample's response rate remains 11.26% [10.59%, 11.91%]. The balanced training subset has 5,568 rows, half yes and half no. It is a changed training distribution, not a new measurement of the bank's response rate.

The balanced fit's average prediction is far too high for this test population. Its AUC is also allowed to change because it was retrained after most negative examples were discarded. In contrast, applying the correction to its fixed predictions preserves their order and therefore preserves AUC exactly.

The correction is optional mathematics, not a general calibration guarantee. It uses the known fraction of negative training examples retained: w = 0.126961. For a balanced-model score q, the adjusted probability is w*q/(w*q+1-q). This undoes the known sampling change in the odds under random sampling within each class; model error can remain. A separate calibration model would have to be fitted on validation data, not on the final-test labels.

What the calling recommendation can and cannot say

A high predicted response does not show that making a call causes an extra subscription. Some people might subscribe without it. These data do not provide the no-call comparison needed to estimate that difference.

For a deliberately hypothetical calculation, suppose calling changes subscription probability from 28% without a call to 30% with one. If an additional subscription contributes $66 and a call costs $6.20, the extra expected contribution is:

(30% − 28%) × $66 − $6.20 = −$4.88 per call.

Every number in that example is an assumption for illustration, not a finding from the bank data. Multiplying 30% by $66 and subtracting call cost would answer a different question because it counts subscriptions that would happen anyway. A randomized call-versus-no-call pilot, where feasible, can help measure the added response. The prediction model may help design that pilot; it does not replace it.

Write a short recommendation that states the frozen model choice, its historical test performance, what the comparison with the simpler model established, and what still needs testing before relying on its probabilities or claiming outreach profit.
